New to Bittensor?

Bittensor is a network of specialized markets called subnets. Miners provide work, and validators assess it under each subnet’s rules. TAO is the network token; alpha is a subnet’s own token. Emissions are newly issued tokens that help fund participation. Daily TAO emissions are injected into each subnet’s liquidity pool (and fund protocol alpha buys) based on its emission share, while subnet owners, validators and miners are paid in liquid alpha. A halving reduces the issuance rate. Customer revenue is separate: it comes from people paying for a service. Customer payments can create demand for alpha through mechanisms such as buybacks and burns.

01

Bittensor in 2026: why the network changed how it pays subnets

In short. The 2026 rules tied each subnet’s emissions to demand for its token. Estimated customer revenue has risen, though emissions still fund most of the network’s early-stage buildout.

Turning subsidized work into a paying business takes different amounts of time across markets. An inference provider can start billing as soon as its API works. A drug-discovery team may spend years testing candidates before a pharmaceutical company licenses one. Both need funding while they build, and both compete for the network’s emissions.

Our February report looked at which subnets had signed customers. Those deals gave us one way to assess demand for the products. Bittensor’s old funding rules gave teams a different signal. Under TaoFlow, a team could trade its own token to lift its share of emissions before it had a product to sell. Root stakers could earn yield without choosing which subnets to back.

Because Bittensor’s daily emissions are fixed, a larger share for one subnet leaves less for everyone else. Under TaoFlow, a team could bid up its own token to capture more emissions even without a paying customer for its product. The July reforms were designed to curb that loop by tying emissions to sustained token price share rather than short-term trading flows.

The 2026 upgrades took on three questions in turn: who deserves the subsidy (the Emission Gate and Root Reborn), who gets paid inside each subnet (eTAO), and how customer cash reaches the token (Gamma).

The July reforms

July’s reforms linked emissions more closely to token price. The Emission Gate reduces a subnet’s allocation when its share of total subnet token prices falls below a threshold. Root Reborn changed what network-wide stakers earn. Their yield now comes from baskets of subnet tokens chosen by validators.

That choice matters because of how much TAO sits in root. On July 24, root held about 5.4 million TAO, nearly 48% of all TAO issued at the time. Stakers can still leave the choice of subnets to a validator, but the tokens in that validator’s basket now shape the yield they receive.

A first way to bet against a subnet

In October, eTAO let each subnet owner set how emissions split between the owner, validators and miners, within limits.

The July rules still read demand through the token market. A subnet can attract buyers for its token before it attracts buyers for its product. Gamma, introduced in November, connects those two markets at the point of sale. Customers can pay with dollar-valued service credits created by burning subnet tokens. It went live on November 9, and commercial uptake is still unproven.

What changed in practice

The first visible result was how emissions were distributed. In July’s simulation, applying the gate increased the share going to the eight highest-emitting subnets from 32.8% to 52.7%. The eight largest recipients on November 10 took 57.0% of emissions. That reading shows how concentrated emissions were that day. The live series starts after the upgrade, so we can’t tell how much of that concentration the gate caused.

SubConnect’s annualized index estimate rose from ~$35M in August to ~$70M in November. Expanded coverage may explain part of the rise; the upgrades’ contribution is unproven.

Revenue against what the network pays out

The network pays subnets in two forms. New $TAO goes into subnet liquidity pools: at $222.94 per TAO (Nov 9 close), a year of issuance was worth about $293M, roughly four times SubConnect’s November estimate. Miners, validators and owners are paid in alpha emissions, each subnet’s own token, which have not yet had their own halving.

Subnet Summer estimates annualized sellable alpha emissions at about $535M on its November 16 snapshot, roughly 7.6 times SubConnect’s revenue estimate. Some recipients hold or stake their rewards, so the figure does not measure actual selling.

Neither ratio is a break-even target. Traditional venture-backed AI startups also spend far more capital than they earn in their early years, though with one key difference: venture losses fall on investors who chose to fund a company, whereas emissions dilute every token holder. The profiles that follow track how teams are using that runway to win paying customers and connect revenue to their tokens.

Customer spending therefore matters to the network’s long-term economics. Some subnets sell services customers already use; others run research that needs years to reach a market.

Protocol release notes and TAO supply

Conviction. Locking a subnet’s alpha builds conviction over time. Once the subnet is at least a year old, ownership can transfer if the highest-conviction hotkey exceeds 18% of the subnet’s eligible alpha. Protocol rules.

Emission Gate mechanics. The gate scales emissions using s³ ÷ (s³ + θ³), where s is the subnet’s smoothed price share after the miner-burn penalty and θ is the threshold, recalculated every 360 blocks. At the threshold a subnet keeps half its linear share. Root can adjust the threshold.

Mechanism Design

Metanova runs three open competition tracks:

  • Small molecules
  • Chemical-search algorithms
  • Nanobodies

Metanova began with single-target screening. The team now describes a search space of 678.5 billion possible molecules, Boltz-2 scoring and a four-target rule for beating the incumbent.

Competitive Landscape

Isomorphic’s reported $3B+ pharma deals show the potential commercial route. Niome and Minos instead work in genomics.

Response to the 2026 rules

Metanova cut its miner burn to 0% on Jun 24, citing the new incentives: under price-based emissions, burning miner emission lowers the price share that sets a subnet’s emissions. Under eTAO it plans a 26% owner / 41% validator / 33% miner split, using the owner share to fund experimental data. The test is what lab work that allocation delivers.

How value reaches alpha

Metanova lists licensing, co-development and paid access to its discovery platform as potential ways to earn revenue. In its public materials, we found no disclosed licensing deal or policy for directing commercial proceeds to SN68 alpha.

On-chain data for Nov 18 shows an 18.5% conviction lock, none of it from the owner’s wallet. The total is above 18%, but it shows team commitment only if the team holds locked tokens in a separate wallet, which it has yet to clarify.

Risk Factors

Drug development takes years and most candidates fail. Search results are team-reported; owner-lock totals differ across trackers.

Takeaway

Metanova has moved from computer-based screening into partner lab testing, with early lab results in hand. Its value to alpha holders depends on a licensing route that has not yet been disclosed.

What to prove next

Validate a candidate for its intended use, secure a licensing deal, and disclose how proceeds reach SN68.

The engineering buyer

An engineering team considering a faster AI model needs to know whether it can rely on the model for the job at hand. The team shares its operating conditions and reference data. Carbon tests the model against that data and reports where its predictions hold up, where they break down, and where more simulations or experiments would help the team decide.

Fast surrogate models can cut simulation times from hours to seconds across batteries, motors and chip cooling, yet hardware teams rarely deploy a model they cannot audit. If Carbon becomes a trusted validation layer, it gives engineering teams a way to adopt faster physics AI without taking a black-box result on faith.

2.3 Compute: where the revenue sits

Research teams need time to turn results into products. Compute already has an established market: customers rent GPUs and pay for model responses. Buyers can compare prices across providers, and compute and infrastructure account for 78–82% of SubConnect’s estimated subnet revenue.

Lium reported $2.32M in October renter spending. Its published 5% platform fee implies about $116K in fees that month. The distinction matters when comparing a rental marketplace with a provider that bills customers directly.

Targon reported burning SN4 alpha worth 8,450 TAO in the last week of October. It attributed 1,772 TAO worth of that burn to purchases funded by organic revenue. Chutes gives us a third view of compute economics because its revenue and pool issuance can be compared over nearly the same dates.

2.4 Inference: Engy (SN53), and Chutes' issuance against revenue

Low prices can bring customers to an inference subnet. Reliability, privacy and verification can give them reasons to stay. Chutes shows how much revenue a subnet can earn from serving models and how that compares with what Bittensor still issues to its pool.

Issuance against revenue at Chutes

Over the August–September period shown, Bittensor issued about $7 worth of TAO to Chutes’ pool for every $1 Chutes earned in estimated revenue, using bittensor.ai’s average emission share for the period. A second tracker records about half that share, which gives roughly $4. Either way, issuance was several times revenue. Chutes’ costs are unknown.

Monthly revenue fell between our two reports: DefiLlama tracked about $464K in February 2026 and $388K in August.

How Chutes is trying to grow paid usage

Chutes started by cutting free and unprofitable usage, tightening subscriptions and putting more emphasis on pay-as-you-go customers. It has since widened distribution and added models. SayGM began offering Chutes models in October. In November, Chutes added Tencent’s Hy4 model to its API, and Chutes announced a partnership with Alibaba Cloud to expand its model lineup.

These launches give developers more ways to use Chutes. The revenue figures above predate them, so their effect on paid usage is still open.

What Engy is building

Engy wants developers to buy inference from a network of independent GPU operators through a single API. A team building a support assistant can choose an open-weight model, send it requests and pay for the tokens it uses. Engy routes each request to an operator running that model. The developer can also ask for proof that the requested model generated the response.

As open-weight models narrow the gap with proprietary labs, inference is becoming a high-volume market where enterprise buyers still worry about silent quantization or model substitution. If Engy’s proofs work reliably at low overhead, they let production teams use cheaper open-market GPUs without sacrificing auditability.

Engy counts tokens from successful paid requests when scoring miners, so serving work customers pay for can earn an operator a larger share of rewards. On the revenue side, Engy has used customer payments to buy and burn SN53 alpha.

That scoring may change. A proposal in Engy’s repository (PR #62) would base 80% of miner rewards on uptime and 20% on paid traffic, rewarding operators for staying available as well as for serving billed requests. The proposal remained pending as of Nov 20, with no activation date announced.

Subnet Context

SubConnect named Engy among the drivers of network revenue growth. Token volume is rising, but paid revenue, not volume, is what funds burns.

What REAX sells

A support agent could use REAX to decide whether to answer a request, send it to a specialist or hand it to a person. A general-purpose LLM answers in open text. A decision model returns a probability for each option, for example 0.82 answer, 0.15 specialist, 0.03 person.

Software can act on those numbers directly. It can set rules such as handing anything below 0.70 to a person, without parsing a reply or guessing how confident the model is.

The narrow task lets the model stay small, which keeps each call fast and cheap. On Decision Models, the s1-fast peer-to-peer tier starts at $0.032 per 1M input tokens; other models and tiers are priced separately. Fixed outputs also let validators recompute the same probabilities to check miners.

The API is compatible with TypeSafe’s Jev service, now supports image-based decisions (DecisionModels), and powers Trishool’s HaloGuard security product (team-reported).

That architecture targets a growing slice of enterprise AI spend: lightweight classifiers and guardrails sitting in front of frontier LLMs to route tickets, filter prompts and flag policy violations. Because those checks run on every interaction, a fast, low-cost decision layer can compound across millions of automated calls even at fractions of a cent per request.

On November 6, REAX reported a Decision Index score of 73.98 against 73.37 for Jev. The 0.61-point lead leaves open how the models compare on a customer’s own requests. REAX says customers have paid since launch, and its founder says one made 1.7M requests in a week. The team hasn’t disclosed revenue.

Disclose recurring paid usage and revenue, then demonstrate a policy and transaction connecting customer payments to alpha.

What do these purchases mean for token holders?

Pool depth limits what a buyback does to price. Daily protocol liquidity makes the largest pools unusually deep: at the Exploit convention, Yuma said top pools carry too much liquidity relative to trading volume (Yuma on X), and TAO Daily noted that a 1,000 $TAO sell on Chutes moves its price by roughly 1%. The depth that absorbs sales also absorbs purchases, while thin long-tail pools move sharply both ways.

Two proposals target pool depth: Yuma’s ASMR proposal would recycle liquidity out of pools, and Bittensor’s planned lending and borrowing upgrade, which Const described as directionally aligned with ASMR (Const on X), would change borrowing mechanics. Neither had shipped as of Nov 20.

The clearest documented responses to the new rules concern how teams allocate rewards. Vanta set its owner share to 0%, REAX reported 26%, and Metanova planned a 26% owner share to fund experimental data. Metanova also publicly linked its June 24 cut to miner burn to the new incentives.

Engy’s revenue-funded burns began about two weeks after the Emission Gate, and SubConnect counted 14 subnets funding buybacks by August 26. Neither source establishes that the gate caused those decisions. Changes to reward settings and payment mechanisms are documented. Their effect on customer growth remains uncertain.

2.8 Who has locked their tokens?

Conviction locks became a confidence signal after July’s upgrade (Opentensor on X) and investor caution following Covenant AI’s April 2026 exit. Under the protocol rules, ownership can transfer only once a subnet is at least a year old, and only if a single hotkey’s conviction exceeds 18% of eligible alpha. Conviction also builds over time, so a new lock counts for less than a mature one. Table 11 shows total locks, not the largest single hotkey, so a total above 18% does not by itself show that a subnet is protected or exposed.

What the totals can show is commitment. Alpha locked from the owner’s wallet ties up the team’s own tokens. A lock funded by community holders says more about their confidence than the team’s, and it does not stop an owner from selling.

Catalysts & Risk Factors

In short. Four signals would show customer demand catching up with issuance in 2027: comparable revenue growth, repeat use, documented token purchases and independent research validation.

Into 2027

Can customer revenue catch up with issuance?

Signs of progress
  1. Comparable growth. SubConnect reports higher revenue across the same set of subnets.
  2. Repeat use. Unaffiliated customers come back to pay for the same subnet services, including through Gamma credits.
  3. Documented token purchases. More subnets show customer revenue funding buybacks, with the customer share broken out.
  4. Independent validation. Research subnets clear milestones partners can reproduce, such as Metanova’s in-vitro results.
Risks to watch
  1. Customer revenue grows more slowly than the dollar value of TAO issuance.
  2. Concentration: revenue stays in compute, where inference is crowded.
  3. Governance: Root Reborn and Null Consensus put allocation and scoring in fewer hands.
  4. Builder exit: teams below the bar have less reason to stay.
  5. Liquidity: deep top pools mute the price impact of buybacks, while thin long-tail pools amplify exits.
What would change our mind
  • The revenue index flattens or falls over the next two readings.
  • Issuance per dollar of revenue rises on matched periods while the $TAO price holds.
  • No non-compute subnet discloses repeat revenue from unaffiliated customers in 2027 (token link tracked separately).

Closing Thesis

Bittensor’s 2026 upgrades changed incentives fast. Within weeks, teams reset reward splits, adjusted burns and gained a native way for customers to pay in subnet tokens.

The profiles show that individual pieces of a commercial economy can work on Bittensor: customer payments funding burns, rewards tracking paid demand, and open research reaching partner labs. None yet combines disclosed revenue, customer-funded token purchases and verified owner locks, and emissions still exceed estimated revenue several times over.

The test for 2027 is whether those pieces converge: repeat customer spending across a comparable set of subnets, research milestones that independent partners reproduce, and clear public records tracing customer payments to alpha. Until they do, Bittensor remains primarily a subsidized R&D market rather than a self-funding commercial network; seeing them appear outside compute would show the model can diversify beyond infrastructure.
